What does Maine face during the winter?

The storms

Nor’easters are the big ones. These low-pressure systems travel up the Atlantic coast and pull ocean moisture inland, bringing heavy snow and strong northeast winds. Wet, heavy snow clings to branches and wires, freezing rain coats everything in ice, and some storms are mostly wind and rain.

Forests cover about 89% of Maine’s land, more than any other state, so most power lines run through trees. When snow, ice or wind bring limbs down, they take the lines with them. Rural lines run for miles, and crews usually restore the repairs that bring back the most customers first. Small towns and back roads tend to see longer waits.

The dangers

Most furnaces, including oil and propane, need electricity, or homes go cold. After a 1998 ice storm, Maine CDC found that a generator in a basement or garage could raise the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning up to 300-fold. Other risks concern medical equipment and refrigerated medicine, and phones and internet can go down with the power.

The risk is highest for older adults, people with disabilities or home medical equipment, households that can’t afford a generator or a hotel, people without a car, and rural residents far from a warming center.
